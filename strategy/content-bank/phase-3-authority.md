### Post 25 · 🖼️ Mon explainer: "FDA-cleared AI" — what it means and doesn't (Pillar B)

**Slide-by-slide:**
1. **"FDA-cleared AI." Sounds bulletproof. Here's what it actually means — and doesn't.**
2. First, the landscape (as I understand current US rules): hundreds of AI medical tools have FDA clearance — overwhelmingly in imaging. So regulated clinical AI is very real and growing fast.
3. **"Cleared" usually means the 510(k) path:** the maker showed the device is substantially equivalent to an existing one. It's meaningful review — but it is *not* a big clinical trial, and it's not "FDA-approved," which is a heavier bar.
4. Also worth knowing: much of the AI in medicine doesn't need FDA review at all. Scribes, scheduling, billing, many "assistive" tools — outside the device lane. The most-used AI in your clinic has likely never met a regulator.
5. The genuinely hard problem regulators are wrestling with: AI that *updates*. A drug is the same molecule forever; a model can shift with new data. Rules for that are still being written.
6. So when you see "FDA-cleared AI": real signal, specific meaning, not a halo. And "no clearance" isn't a scandal — it may just be a different category of tool.
7. **The literate question isn't "is it FDA-approved?" — it's "what claim was reviewed, and what evidence backs *this* use?"** 🔖 Save for vendor meetings.

**Caption:**
> "FDA-cleared" ≠ "FDA-approved" ≠ "unregulated" — and knowing the difference is basic AI literacy for anyone in healthcare. Clearance (usually 510(k)) is a real, specific review; much clinical AI sits outside the device lane entirely; and adaptive models are the open frontier. Ask what claim was reviewed, not whether there's a halo. (Educational, as I understand current US rules — not legal advice.)

### Post 28 · 🖼️ Mon explainer: 5 skills that transfer from medicine to product (Pillar D)

**Slide-by-slide:**
1. **Doctors already have product skills. Most just don't know the translation. Here are 5.**
2. **Differential diagnosis → debugging.** Generate hypotheses, rank by likelihood and danger, test cheaply first, never anchor on the first answer. That's literally troubleshooting a system.
3. **Informed consent → honest UX.** Explaining risk/benefit to a scared patient teaches you to present tradeoffs without manipulation. Half of product design is exactly this.
4. **Triage → prioritization.** Sickest first, not loudest first. Every backlog needs someone who can tell "urgent" from "shouting."
5. **The clinical exam → user research.** Patients don't present textbook answers; users don't either. Doctors are trained to hear what's *not* said and probe gently. That's elite interviewing.
6. **M&M conferences → blameless postmortems.** Medicine institutionalized reviewing bad outcomes to fix systems, not people — decades before tech called it that.

### Post 31 · 🖼️ Mon explainer: How to evaluate ANY health AI headline (Pillar B — evergreen closer)

**Slide-by-slide:**
1. **"AI beats doctors at X." Before you share that headline, run this 60-second checklist.**
2. **Check 1 — Beats doctors at *what exactly*?** Answering exam questions ≠ practicing medicine. A benchmark is a photograph of one skill in perfect lighting.
3. **Check 2 — Compared to *which* doctors, doing what?** Rushed residents at 3am or specialists with full workups? The comparison condition is half the result.
4. **Check 3 — Retrospective or real world?** Curated past data is a rehearsal. The performance that matters is live, messy, and prospective. Most headlines are rehearsals.
5. **Check 4 — Who's accountable when it's wrong?** A tool with no liability answer isn't finished, no matter the accuracy. Someone signs the chart.
6. **Check 5 — Who paid, and what are they selling?** Not cynicism — context. Vendor studies aren't lies; they're best-case lighting. Adjust accordingly.
7. Pass all five and it might be a genuinely important result — those exist and I get excited about them too! The point is a filter, not a fortress.
8. **Save this checklist. You'll need it roughly weekly at the current pace of headlines.** 🔖

**Caption:**
> A 60-second filter for every "AI beats doctors" headline: beats them at what, compared to whom, retrospective or live, who's liable when it's wrong, and who funded it. Not cynicism — literacy. Save it; the headlines aren't slowing down.
