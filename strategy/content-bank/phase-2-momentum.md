### Post 10 · 🖼️ Mon explainer: The 4 kinds of healthcare AI (a map) (Pillar B)

**Slide-by-slide:**
1. **"Healthcare AI" is four different industries wearing one name. Here's the map.**
2. **1 — Back-office AI.** Coding, billing, prior auth, scheduling. Least sexy, most deployed, quietly enormous. Your hospital already uses this.

### Post 11 · 🎬 Wed Reel: Why "accuracy" is the wrong question (Pillar A)

**Script (~55s):**
> **[HOOK]** A clinical AI tool tells you it's 95% accurate. As a doctor who builds these: that number tells me almost nothing. Here's what I ask instead.
>
> **[1]** *Accurate on whom?* 95% on the dataset it trained on — or on patients like mine? Models travel badly. A tool tuned in one health system can stumble in the next one over.
>
> **[2]** *What kind of wrong?* A tool that misses rare-but-lethal cases and one that over-flags everything can have the *same* accuracy. One kills quietly. The other buries clinicians in alerts until they tune it out — which also kills quietly.
>
> **[3]** *Does the workflow catch the errors?* The real safety of a clinical AI system is model + human + workflow. A weaker model with a great review loop beats a stronger model everyone rubber-stamps.
>
> **[CLOSE]** So: not "how accurate is it?" but "how does it fail, and who catches it?" That's the grown-up question. Follow for more.

**Caption:**
> "95% accurate" is a marketing sentence, not a safety claim. The questions that matter for clinical AI: accurate on whom, wrong in which direction, and caught by what workflow. Model + human + process is the unit of safety — not the model alone.
>
> #clinicalAI #patientsafety #healthcareAI #AIinmedicine #healthtech #medtwitter #machinelearning

### Post 13 · 🖼️ Mon explainer: What happens to your data when a hospital "uses AI" (Pillar B)

**Slide-by-slide:**
1. **"My hospital uses AI." Where does your data actually go? Let's walk it through.**
2. First, the boring good news: health data is covered by HIPAA. Vendors that touch it sign agreements (BAAs) making them legally on the hook too. "Uses AI" ≠ "your chart is on the open internet."
3. Typical path: your record stays in the hospital's systems → a vetted vendor processes it under contract → results come back into your chart. Reputable tools don't train public models on your identifiable data.
4. The honest caveats: de-identification is strong but imperfect. Contracts vary. And enforcement lags innovation — as I understand the current rules, they were written for a pre-AI world.
5. Questions you're allowed to ask your clinic: Is my data used to train models? Can I opt out of AI-assisted tools? Who reviews the AI's output? (A good clinic can answer all three.)
6. My take as someone who builds in this space: the industry norm is much more careful than people fear — and less careful than it should ultimately be. Both things are true.
7. **Informed beats anxious. Ask the three questions.** 🔖 Save for your next appointment.

**Caption:**
> Where your data goes when a hospital "uses AI," in plain English: HIPAA still applies, vendors sign liability-sharing contracts, and reputable tools don't train on your identifiable data — plus the honest caveats about de-identification and lagging rules. ### Post 16 · 🖼️ Mon explainer: Why pilots die (the graveyard slide) (Pillar B)

**Slide-by-slide:**
1. **Most hospital AI pilots die. Not because the AI is bad. Here's the actual failure list.**
2. **Death #1: No workflow owner.** The pilot "belongs" to innovation, IT, *and* the department — which means nobody. Tools without an owner drift for six months and expire quietly.
3. **Death #2: Wrong success metric.** "Clinicians liked it" doesn't renew a contract. If nobody defined the number — time saved, gaps closed, dollars — the pilot can't *win*, only end.
4. **Death #3: The champion leaves.** One enthusiastic doctor carried the whole thing. They took a new job. RIP, pilot.
5. **Death #4: It worked, and nothing happened.** The pilot hit its numbers but there was no budget line, no rollout plan, no exec sponsor for scale. Success with no next step is still death.
6. **Death #5: The integration was a demo, not a build.** It ran beside the EHR instead of inside it. "We'll integrate later" is where pilots go to sleep forever.
7. The pattern: pilots die of *organizational* causes, not technical ones. The model was fine. The org chart wasn't.
8. **If you're piloting AI: name an owner, a number, and a scale plan before day one — or you're just scheduling a funeral.** 🔖 Save this checklist.

**Caption:**
> The hospital AI pilot graveyard, categorized: no workflow owner, no success metric, champion turnover, success-with-no-scale-plan, and demo-grade integration. Almost every death is organizational, not technical. Name an owner, a number, and a scale plan before day one.

### Post 19 · 🖼️ Mon explainer: Why your doctor stares at the screen (Pillar B)

**Slide-by-slide:**
1. **Your doctor isn't ignoring you. They're feeding the machine. Here's how it got this way.**
2. For every hour with patients, clinicians spend up to *two* on documentation and admin. That stat isn't laziness or bad typing — it's structural.
3. How we got here: electronic records were mandated and built for *billing and compliance* first, care second. Every regulation and lawsuit added a click. Nobody ever removed one.
4. The result: the note became a legal-financial document that happens to contain medicine. The screen won the exam room not because doctors chose it — but because the system pays for what's typed, not what's said.
5. This is the actual origin story of clinical AI. Scribes, inbox drafts, summarizers — they exist because the documentation burden became unbearable enough to fund an industry.
6. The hopeful version: AI absorbs the machine-feeding, and the visit becomes a conversation again. The cautionary version: we use the saved time to cram in more visits. The tech allows both futures — leadership picks one.
7. **Next time your doctor apologizes for the screen — the system built that screen. And a lot of us are working on tearing it down.** 🔖 Share with someone who complains about this.

**Caption:**
> Why your doctor stares at the screen: records systems built for billing first, decades of added clicks, and a note that became a legal document with some medicine inside. That burden is the real origin story of clinical AI — and whether the saved time goes back to patients is a leadership choice, not a tech one.
