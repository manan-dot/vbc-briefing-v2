### Post 2 · 🖼️ Explainer: Value-based care in 60 seconds (Pillar B)

**Slot:** Wed or Thu, Week 2

**Slide-by-slide:**
1. **Your doctor gets paid for visits, not results. That's finally changing.**
2. Most of US healthcare runs on fee-for-service: every visit, test, and procedure = a bill. More care → more revenue. Whether or not you get *better* is beside the point.
3. **Value-based care flips it:** clinicians get paid for outcomes — keeping you healthy, out of the hospital, managing the chronic stuff well.
4. Sounds obviously better. So why isn't it everywhere? Because measuring "health" is brutally hard. It takes data from everywhere, risk math, and closing hundreds of small care gaps per patient.
5. No human team can track all of that manually. That's the honest reason AI matters in healthcare — not robot doctors. **Boring, load-bearing infrastructure.**
6. AI that finds the patient who missed a screening. Flags the rising-risk diabetic. Drafts the note so the visit is about the patient, not the keyboard.
7. **Takeaway: the future of healthcare AI isn't dramatic. It's the quiet machinery that makes paying-for-health actually work.** 🔖 Save this for the next time someone says "AI will replace doctors."

**Caption:**
> Value-based care, explained simply: instead of paying for visits and procedures, we pay for keeping people healthy — and AI is the only way the math scales. This is the corner of healthcare I work in, and it's where AI is quietly most useful. Save this one. 📌
>

### Post 7 · 🖼️ Mon explainer: Ambient AI scribes without buzzwords (Pillar B)

**Slide-by-slide:**
1. **Your doctor might stop typing during your visit. Here's the machinery behind that.**
2. "Ambient AI scribe" = an app listens to the visit (with consent), transcribes it, and drafts the clinical note before the doctor opens the chart.
3. Step 1: speech-to-text tuned for medical vocabulary — "metoprolol," not "metro polo." Step 2: separating who said what.
4. Step 3 is the actual magic *and* the actual risk: turning 20 minutes of messy human conversation into a structured note — what's relevant, what's billable, what's clinically load-bearing.
5. The doctor is supposed to review and edit every draft. The honest tension: a great draft gets a careful read for the first month… and a skim by month three. That's automation bias, and it's the thing to watch.
6. Why this matters to you as a patient: done well, your doctor looks at *you* instead of the screen. Done lazily, errors ride the draft into your chart.
7. **Good clinical AI isn't the kind that writes the note. It's the kind that makes the review easy and the errors loud.** 🔖 Save to explain to a colleague.

**Script (~50s):**

>
> **[1]** When reviewing the draft takes as long as writing it. If I have to fact-check every line against my memory of the visit, you didn't save me time — you changed my job from author to auditor. Auditing is *harder*.

> **[2]** When the time saved gets refilled. Ten minutes saved per note has a way of becoming two extra appointment slots. The clock didn't get lighter; the day got denser. That's a leadership choice, not a technology fact — but tell me which one your rollout made.

> **[3]** When the tool only fits the easy visits. Simple follow-up? Flawless. Complex multi-problem visit — the one that actually causes the 11pm charting? That's where it quietly gives up.
